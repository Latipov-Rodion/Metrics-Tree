Gross margin vs contribution margin — what's the difference and which one to use

An online store with a 46% gross margin looks healthy. But after shipping, payment fees, packaging and returns, each $100 of revenue leaves not $46 but $32. Subtract advertising and it's $17. That gap decides what CAC and what ROAS you can afford. Gross margin and contribution margin are often used interchangeably, but they answer different questions.

Definitions and formulas

Gross Margin = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue × 100%

COGS (cost of goods sold) is the direct cost of the product: purchased inventory and inbound freight for a store, hosting and support for SaaS. Gross margin shows how profitable the product itself is.

Contribution Margin = (Revenue − Variable costs) / Revenue × 100%

Variable costs are everything that grows with each sale: COGS plus outbound shipping, payment fees, packaging, returns, marketplace and partner commissions. Contribution margin shows how much each sale contributes toward fixed costs and profit.

Fixed costs — rent, engineering and office salaries, shared tools — are in neither metric.

Exactly where the line between COGS and other variable costs falls depends on accounting policy: some companies include shipping in COGS. What matters is choosing a rule and sticking to it.

Worked example: an online store

One month:

  • Revenue (net of returns): $100,000
  • COGS (inventory): $54,000
  • Outbound shipping: $8,000
  • Payment processing: $2,500
  • Packaging: $1,500
  • Returns handling: $2,000
  • Performance advertising: $15,000

Gross margin = (100,000 − 54,000) / 100,000 = 46%
Variable costs before ads = 54,000 + 8,000 + 2,500 + 1,500 + 2,000 = $68,000
Contribution margin = (100,000 − 68,000) / 100,000 = 32%
Contribution margin after advertising = (32,000 − 15,000) / 100,000 = 17%

By MetricTree's thresholds:

  • Gross margin of 46% is "good" for e-commerce (>45%).
  • Contribution margin of 32% is "average" on the universal scale (20–40%). The guideline for fashion is 35–55%, for electronics 15–30%.
  • After ads, 17% is left — on the same scale that's already the "poor" zone (<20%). A store with a "good" gross margin earns less per sale than it seems.

Why it matters: break-even ROAS

The minimum ROAS at which advertising pays for itself is calculated from margin:

Break-even ROAS = 1 / Margin

Which margin you useBreak-even ROAS
Gross margin 46%2.17x
Contribution margin 32%3.13x
If a marketer works from gross margin, a campaign at 2.5x ROAS looks profitable. In reality it loses money: each ad dollar brings back $2.50 of revenue, and after all variable costs 2.5 × 0.32 = $0.80 is left. For decisions about ads and CAC you need contribution margin.

Worked example: SaaS

  • MRR: $50,000
  • Hosting: $4,000, support: $3,000, third-party APIs: $1,500 → COGS = $8,500
  • Payment fees: $1,500

Gross margin = (50,000 − 8,500) / 50,000 = 83% — "good" for SaaS (75–85%).
Contribution margin = (50,000 − 8,500 − 1,500) / 50,000 = 80% — within the typical SaaS range of 70–85%.

In SaaS the two metrics nearly coincide: there are few variable costs outside COGS. In e-commerce and marketplaces a 10–20 percentage-point gap is normal.

Common mistakes

1. Calculating e-commerce LTV on gross margin

LTV = customer revenue × margin. Use 46% instead of 32% and LTV is inflated by almost 1.5x — and affordable CAC along with it.

2. Forgetting returns

In fashion, returns can eat a noticeable share of revenue. Calculate margin on net revenue and include the cost of handling returns in variable costs.

3. One margin for the whole catalog

Ads often sell the cheapest, lowest-margin products. The store's average margin overstates how well those campaigns pay back.

4. Mixing fixed and variable costs

Warehouse staff on a fixed salary are a fixed cost; a per-order picking fee is variable. This determines how margin behaves as you grow.

5. Comparing gross margins across companies without checking accounting policy

One company puts shipping in COGS, another doesn't. When comparing with the market, check what's included in cost of goods.

Bottom line

Gross margin tells you whether the product is good. Contribution margin tells you how much each sale actually earns — and that's what break-even ROAS, affordable CAC and LTV should be based on. In SaaS the difference is small; in e-commerce it can flip your conclusions.
