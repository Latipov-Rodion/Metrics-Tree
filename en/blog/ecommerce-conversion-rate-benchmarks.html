E-commerce conversion rate — benchmarks for every funnel step and how to calculate it

"What's a good conversion rate for an online store?" is one of the most common questions in e-commerce. The short answer: 1–4% of sessions end in an order. The long answer is more useful: a single top-line number tells you almost nothing about where you lose shoppers. You need to look at the funnel step by step.

Formula

Conversion rate (CR) = Orders / Sessions × 100%

Some teams calculate it per user rather than per session — the number is then higher, because one person visits several times before buying. What matters is choosing one definition and sticking to it.

The /en/cr calculator computes the base version: conversions / visitors.

The e-commerce funnel, step by step

A typical online store funnel:

  • Session → a visitor lands on the site
  • Product page view
  • Add to cart
  • Checkout started
  • Order

Each step has its own conversion and its own reasons for drop-off: at the top — irrelevant traffic and slow pages; on the product page — photos, price and reviews; in the cart — shipping cost and surprise fees; at checkout — long forms and forced account creation.

Worked example

A store, one month:

StepCountStep conversionOf sessions
Sessions100,000—100%
Product page views55,00055%55%
Add to cart8,00014.5%8%
Checkout started4,00050%4%
Orders2,20055%2.2%
CR = 2,200 / 100,000 = 2.2% — within the normal range for e-commerce.

Cart abandonment = 1 − 2,200 / 8,000 = 72.5% — around the market average, slightly worse than 70%.

With a $60 average order value, revenue = 2,200 × $60 = $132,000.

Now a scenario: remove the shipping-cost surprise (show it on the product page) and enable guest checkout. Say cart abandonment drops to 65%:

Orders = 8,000 × 0.35 = 2,800 → CR = 2.8%, revenue = $168,000 (+$36,000, +27%) — without spending a single extra dollar on traffic.

E-commerce benchmarks

The thresholds MetricTree uses for the e-commerce industry:

MetricPoorNormalGood
Conversion rate (CR)<1%1–4%>4%
Cart abandonment>75% (alarming)~70% (average)<60%
Bounce rate>55%30–55%<30%
Repeat purchase rate<10%20–30%30–50% (>50% excellent)
Cart abandonment by category: fashion 68–72%, beauty 65–70%, electronics 75–82%. Mobile web abandonment runs higher than desktop: 78–85% is typical.

The ~70% average is in line with the Baymard Institute's long-running research — the most cited source on cart abandonment and checkout UX. Baymard also breaks down the causes: extra costs at checkout, forced account creation, slow delivery, and long forms.

Why conversion differs several-fold between stores:

  • Order value. Expensive purchases (electronics, furniture) take longer to decide — lower CR, but higher AOV.
  • Traffic mix. Branded and email traffic converts several times better than cold paid social.
  • Device. Mobile traffic usually converts worse than desktop while making up a larger share of sessions.
  • Returning customers. Stores with a high share of returning buyers have higher CR.

Common mistakes

1. Comparing yourself with a different niche

1.5% CR can be excellent in furniture and weak in consumables. Compare with your category's norm and with your own trend.

2. Looking at CR without order value

Conversion gains driven by discounts often come with lower AOV and margin. Track revenue per session = CR × AOV (/en/aov).

3. Calculating cart abandonment from sessions

The correct base is carts created, not all visits. Otherwise you mix cart problems with product page and traffic problems. Calculator — /en/cartAbandonment.

4. Not filtering bots and internal traffic

Bots, employees and test orders distort CR in either direction. Filter them out in analytics before calculating.

5. Averaging all sources and devices

A 2.2% average can be 4% on desktop and 1.2% on mobile. A mobile checkout problem stays invisible in the average.

6. Ignoring seasonality

Conversion in November during sales and in February are different markets. Compare year over year or against a rolling average.

What raises conversion fastest

  • Transparent shipping cost before the cart — the top reason for abandoned carts.
  • Guest checkout with no forced registration.
  • Short forms and address autocomplete, popular payment methods.
  • Load speed of product and cart pages on mobile.
  • Reviews and photos on the product page — they work on the view → cart step.
  • Abandoned cart emails — recover part of the shoppers almost for free.

How to find the bottleneck in your funnel

Compare each step — not the top-line conversion — with its own history and with neighbouring segments:

  • Few product page views with normal traffic — a traffic relevance issue, or catalog navigation and search.
  • Few add-to-carts — price, photos, reviews, size availability, delivery times on the product page.
  • High cart abandonment — unexpected costs, forced registration, too few payment options.
  • Low checkout completion — long forms, validation errors, a slow payment page.

A handy technique is to estimate the potential effect: how many orders you would gain by bringing one step up to the norm. In the example above, cutting cart abandonment by 7.5 percentage points added 27% to revenue. Start with the step where that gain is largest for the smallest cost of change.

FAQ

What is the average e-commerce conversion rate?

Around 1–4% of sessions; below 1% is a reason to look for a problem, above 4% is a good result. It depends heavily on category, order value and traffic source.

Should conversion be calculated per session or per user?

Both are valid. Session-based conversion is lower and more common in web analytics. Just don't mix them when comparing periods.

Why is mobile conversion lower?

Small screens, awkward address and card entry, slower loading, and the habit of browsing on a phone but buying on a computer. Some mobile sessions convert later on another device — so look at cross-device attribution, not only mobile CR.

Bottom line

A normal online store conversion rate is 1–4% and a good one is above 4%, but the top-line number matters less than the step-by-step funnel. Find the step with the biggest gap against the norm, fix it, and measure the effect in revenue per session, not just conversion percentage.
